A place at the edge.
A way to hand work over.

FERB should feel available while you are doing something else. Its interface should take the shape of the moment: speaking, handing over material, waiting, deciding, or receiving a result.

Research and concepts assembled 10 October 2026. This is a proposed direction, supported by precedents and HCI literature, with interactive simulations. Its usability and emotional appeal still need testing with people.

The design hypothesis

A theory of handoff

Edges carry availability.
Objects carry work.
Voice carries intent.

This is our synthesis, not an established psychological law. The test is whether people can delegate, leave, return, and understand what happened with less effort.

A conversation is a sequence of utterances. Delegated work has another useful shape: an instruction, a bounded set of inputs, an evolving task, and a result. Keeping those together gives the person something they can inspect and return to. The notch becomes a threshold between their current activity and the work they have handed over.

Available

A quiet seam at the edge. No invitation that repeatedly competes with the current task.

Invoked

Voice or selected material opens a surface. The person can see exactly what belongs to the request.

Working

The surface contracts. A short status and task-specific stop control stay reachable.

Returned

A brief, file, changed item, or decision appears with its provenance and verification state.

Weiser and Brown’s calm technology supports moving information between peripheral awareness and focused attention. Ishii and Ullmer’s tangible interfaces suggest treating information as something situated and graspable. Bret Victor’s argument for informative graphics suggests that an artifact should answer useful questions before a person has to ask them. These are influences, not evidence that a notch interface will outperform a window. Calm technology · Tangible Bits · Magic Ink.

Three directions, three organizing ideas

Choose the relationship, then refine its appearance

Voice → intent

Veil

A soft listening surface unfolds beneath the hardware. Words become editable intent. Once committed, it recedes; the finished brief arrives as a small object.

Feeling: quiet, close, receptive.
Risk: voice can feel socially awkward or ambiguous in shared spaces. Typing must be equally available.

Try the voice handoff ↗
Material → outcome

Relay

The notch accepts source material. A frosted sleeve holds a bounded request. The returned object carries its own result and source references.

Feeling: useful, tactile, dependable.
Risk: an unfamiliar drop target can be missed. Visible click and keyboard alternatives are necessary.

Hand over the sample notes ↗
Delegation → recall

Thread

Small tabs hold onto distinct pieces of work. Follow a task from result to sources to original request. A question belongs to the task it concerns.

Feeling: continuity, reassurance, orientation.
Risk: many tasks could crowd the edge. Cap visible handles and provide an overflow destination.

Follow the task trail ↗

Recommendation: start with Veil as the main encounter. Prototype Relay’s explicit context handoff inside that direction next. Keep Thread as an alternative to test for people who delegate several tasks at once. Combining all three immediately would conceal which idea is helping.

Human experience, in concrete decisions

The small choices that create the relationship

MomentDesign decisionWhy / what to test
Approaching FERBKeep a consistent location, a keyboard invocation, and a menu-bar fallback. Hover can preview; it must not accidentally begin listening.Availability should be discoverable without turning ordinary pointer movement into an action.
SpeakingExplicit start and stop, a named listening state, and readable captured words. Retain typing. The current prototype plays a visual example only.People need to know what was captured. Waveform amplitude should come from real signal level in the native build.
CorrectingLet the person edit the words before commitment. Keep attached sources visible beside the request.Recover from recognition errors where they occur. Avoid making a correction another conversational turn.
CommittingUse a concrete verb such as “Make brief.” Place the primary action at the trailing edge of the request; keep context beside it.The action label names the consequence. Native layout must adapt to localization and keyboard conventions.
WaitingContract to a short activity label. Keep task-specific Stop reachable. Closing the surface leaves work running.Separate hiding the interface from cancelling a task. Avoid made-up percentages and motion that implies false progress.
Being interruptedShow a question only when the task genuinely needs a decision; explain the conflict and offer the actual alternatives.The person should understand why FERB is interrupting. Expired questions must be visibly invalid.
Receiving workReturn an inspectable artifact with sources and an honest verification label. Keep unverified completion distinct from checked completion.A polished receipt must not overstate correctness. The sample’s “checked” labels describe fictional demo data.
Returning laterAttach the original request and sources to the result. A task trail lets people recover context without replaying messages.Test whether this representation helps after a real interruption, rather than asking whether people like the aesthetic.
Using frostUse milky opacity behind words, a restrained bright edge, and limited blur. Offer solid material and respect reduced transparency.The maker of Alcove describes adapting glass on near-white wallpapers. That is a useful reported technique, not a contrast guarantee for FERB. Source.
Using motionShort transitions originate at the notch. Keyboard interactions are immediate. Reduced motion keeps state changes and removes movement.Animation explains where a surface came from. It should never delay Stop or make a person wait for a control.

Palette choices here are material decisions: pale warm neutrals for the surface, dark ink for reading, a restrained terracotta recording signal, and quieter green for a checked state. These are hypotheses about appearance and hierarchy. There is no claim that one color universally creates trust, calm, or a particular emotion. State names and symbols carry meaning alongside color.

Kurtenbach and Buxton’s marking-menu work is useful for a more specific reason: expert gestures can coexist with visible choices that support learning and recall. FERB should provide a visible equivalent for any drag, hold, or shortcut. The study’s performance findings cannot be transferred to this new interface. Author-hosted paper.

From this study to a real Mac surface

Respect the hardware and FERB’s actual behavior

Geometry

The camera cutout is physically occluded. Text and controls belong below or beside it. Derive available regions from screen geometry instead of hard-coding one MacBook’s dimensions. Use an edge-tab fallback on external displays and test multiple displays, full screen, menu-bar density, and coexistence with other notch utilities. Apple’s NSScreen safe regions are relevant implementation references.

Scope

The notch is the quick encounter. Long documents, settings, and durable history can open in a conventional secondary window. Apple’s Dynamic Island examples inform the representation; they are not a public “Mac Dynamic Island” API. A native AppKit/SwiftUI implementation needs its own panel behavior and accessibility semantics.

Backend truth

The earlier repository inspection found separate outcomes for done, done_unverified, failed, needs_user, and cancelled. Preserve those distinctions. Stopping one task must send its exact root identity; an empty stop selection can mean all tasks. A surface closing must never imply a backend stop.

Durable continuity

The inspected status route marks unseen results shown on retrieval. A native client needs durable result handling and a clear acknowledgement strategy. Question expiry and task identity need to be represented explicitly. Preserve the existing Right Option voice convention unless deliberately changed. File recovery is route-specific, so a universal Undo promise would be misleading.

These prototypes are interaction studies. Voice, source notes, work, and results are simulated. They do not call FERB, record audio, read files, generate PDFs, or establish production readiness. Backend findings come from the earlier local inspection; they must be rechecked against the revision used for integration.

The next evidence we need

Test the encounter, not just the screenshot

Give people the same small task in Veil and Relay. Include a quiet workspace and a shared-space scenario. Observe whether they can identify attached context, correct the instruction, dismiss the surface while work continues, stop only that task, and find the result after switching away. Use Thread in a separate session with several concurrent tasks and an unresolved question.

Record mistaken actions, missing context, time to recover the right result, and participants’ explanations of what FERB is doing. Ask what they expected before revealing the behavior. Include keyboard-only use, VoiceOver, reduced motion, reduced transparency, bright and dark wallpapers, and a display without a notch. This is a proposed evaluation, not research already conducted.
