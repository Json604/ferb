01 / Strategic brief

Make delegation feel settled.

FERB is an assistant that acts across a person’s Mac while they continue working. Its interface has to support a relationship over time: asking, delegating, checking, correcting, remembering, and returning. A long conversation transcript alone does not give unfinished work a dependable place.

The experience promise: “I can leave this with FERB. I know what it has, what it is doing, and what still needs me.”

Initial audience hypothesis: Mac users who work across documents, browsers, messages, and projects; comfortable with software, but unwilling to supervise an agent like a terminal process. This is a design assumption inferred from the product, not an audience study.

Emotional brief: relief, agency, clarity, continuity, then delight. The product should feel capable and considerate. It should reward a person with work completed and attention returned.

What people should remember: FERB did useful work, remembered the right context, and made the important decisions easy to understand. Its material and character should reinforce that experience.

How this study was conducted

Read the repository’s working agreements, README, capability ledger, RPC contract, upgrade plan, and selected outcome, approval, reconnect, stop, and recovery code. Reviewed research and official guidance across HCI, automation, attention, motivation, color, accessibility, and interaction design. Visually inspected the supplied glass demo and the public Things, Raycast, Granola, and Tolan references in a browser; examined Linear and Little Snitch’s published behavior.

Scope honesty: this is desk research and concept design. No FERB daemon task was executed, no new live capability was verified, no user interviews were conducted, and no competitor account was used. Book work uses author/publisher material and an available Calm Technology chapter; it is not a claim to have read every book cover to cover. Some papers were available only as abstracts. Refero was unavailable, so its large screen-library survey was replaced by direct source study. Those limits are marked in the bibliography.

02 / Research translated into design

The human experience begins with control.

There is no single psychological trick that makes an agent feel human. The strongest research gives us questions to answer and failure modes to avoid. The design implications below are proposals for FERB; the cited studies did not test these screens.

Evidence or lensWhat it supportsFERB design implication
Attention & interruptionMark, Gudith and Klocke found that interrupted participants compensated with faster work but reported more stress, effort, frustration and time pressure. This was a particular experiment, not a universal interruption penalty. [6]Keep ordinary progress inside the task. Interrupt when the person can usefully act. Measure disruption as part of product quality.
Calm technologyCase’s published chapter distinguishes central and peripheral attention and argues for unobtrusive status. [4]A quiet menu-bar presence when FERB is working; a purposeful decision surface when attention is needed.
Autonomy & competenceSelf-determination theory identifies autonomy, competence and relatedness as basic psychological needs. Applying it to an agent interface is a design inference. [7]Make scope understandable, correction easy, and stopping immediate to request. Help the person retain authorship of the outcome.
Appropriate relianceLee and See frame trust around reliance suited to actual automation capability. More trust is not automatically better. Abstract reviewed. [8]Show what was checked, what remains uncertain, and what the person should do next. Avoid an invented confidence percentage.
Cognitive offloadingRisko and Gilbert review how people use actions and external supports to reduce cognitive demand. Abstract reviewed. [9]A stable task record can carry the burden of remembering. Preserve task identity across quick panel, main app, notification and history.
Automation handoffsBainbridge’s paper warns that automation can leave humans responsible for difficult abnormal situations. Abstract reviewed. [10]When FERB needs help, restore context before asking: goal, attempted action, current state, consequence of each choice.
Signifiers & feedbackNorman distinguishes possible actions from the visible signals that make those actions discoverable. [1]Use “Review draft,” “Stop this task,” and “Show what changed.” An unlabeled orb or icon cannot carry the main interaction alone.
Human–AI interactionMicrosoft’s guidelines cover capability expectations, contextual timing, correction, uncertainty, feedback, and global control. [11]Design the first use, everyday use, failure, and long-term adaptation together. Keep model and runtime details available on demand.
Mental modelsGoogle PAIR warns that human-like presentation can create expectations beyond system capability. [12]Personality may make FERB approachable. It must still explain what it can access and do. Do not imply omniscience or emotional need.
Color in contextElliot and Maier caution that boundary conditions and real-world generalization matter before strong applied color claims. [16]Choose a coherent palette, then test it with the intended audience. “Green creates trust” is not a valid product argument.

Seven principles to carry into every screen

  1. Keep the person’s goal visible. Show the intended outcome before the process.
  2. Make attention optional until it is necessary. A person should be able to leave while work proceeds.
  3. Show the basis for reassurance. A result and its evidence matter more than an enthusiastic claim.
  4. Let people correct locally. A wrong name, file or remembered preference should be editable where it appears.
  5. Preserve continuity. Returning should not require reconstructing a conversation.
  6. Use warmth in service of clarity. Plain, considerate language beats cute error messages.
  7. Give uncertainty a proper place. It is a normal state of agentic work, with its own useful next actions.

05 / Experience architecture

Three distances from your work.

1. At a glance: a menu-bar item communicates whether FERB is available, working, waiting for you or stopped. It opens a compact status panel. Avoid a permanently pulsing presence, mystery colored dots, and progress-by-animation.

2. Within reach: a quick panel accepts a request, an explicit context attachment, or a voice utterance. It acknowledges delivery and can be dismissed without cancelling the task. The shortcut is configurable and conflict-checked; do not silently replace Spotlight, Raycast or the existing Right Option voice binding.

3. A place to return: the main app holds ongoing work, results, questions, memory, routines and settings. It opens to the most useful continuity point, not automatically to a blank chat.

Ask / attach context→Accepted task→Work in background→Decision if needed→Result + evidence

Proposed information architecture

HistoryFind a result or reconstruct an action.Search by task, date, artifact and source; stable task details with conversation, outcomes and evidence.

RoutinesUnderstand future delegated work.What runs, when, timezone, next run, scope, last outcome, change and stop controls.

Conversation remains an important input and correction channel. The task becomes the durable object: one stable identity, the original words, the authorized scope, the current state, decisions, resulting artifacts, and what was checked. Asking a casual question should still feel simple; it need not produce an elaborate project.

06 / The moments that matter

Design the handoffs end to end.

First use: one understandable success

  1. Explain a concrete job FERB can perform and show one finished example.
  2. Explain access and data processing in plain language before the relevant system permission request. Respect FERB’s decided access model; do not invent a restricted execution tier.
  3. Walk through required setup as named steps with real status and a recovery route to macOS Settings. Optional microphone setup remains separately understandable.
  4. Let the person choose a low-stakes task using a file or context they explicitly provide. Use a real supported route; do not stage a success the product cannot reproduce.
  5. Show the resulting artifact, what was checked, and how to stop or correct future work.

Activation is the person successfully delegating and understanding the result. Connecting every service, choosing a model, creating an agent persona, and touring every tab should not be prerequisites to understanding the product.

Everyday delegation: make the handoff tangible

The input has a visible label, a multiline text field, a context area, optional voice, and one send action. The submitted request stays visible. Show “Sending…” while delivery is unresolved, then an acknowledgment after the daemon accepts it. Keep the composer usable for another request.

For longer work, show the goal, any meaningful scope, and the current human-readable activity. “Checking the draft against your notes” helps orient the person; a stream of internal tool names usually does not. If no new event has arrived, show the last update time instead of inventing busywork or an estimated percentage.

Returning: tell me what changed

On reopen, preserve unfinished decisions and the previous reading position where appropriate. Put genuinely actionable items above routine results. A finished card should contain the result first, a direct artifact link second, and the evidence disclosure third. A notification should open that exact task.

If nothing is waiting, say “Nothing needs you right now.” Give a useful input and a small set of truthful examples. Do not turn quietness into an invitation to manufacture more activity.

A decision: prepare the thing before asking

When the daemon requests confirmation, display the concrete action: recipient and address, sending account, full content, attachments, destination, amount or irreversible consequence as relevant. Keep all supplied options and free text. Preserve exact identity across retries; an approval must not silently authorize an altered payload.

Send this project update to Maya?
To: Maya · maya@example.com
From: Your work account · you@example.com
Subject: Project update · Attachments: None

[The exact message]

Do not send　 Edit　 Send once

Names and addresses above are fictional. Buttons belong below the object being reviewed; the affirmative action goes at the trailing edge in left-to-right Mac layouts. Initial focus should make review possible rather than capture an unrelated Return key and send. A background decision should not steal keyboard focus. Closing the decision view leaves the pending question unresolved until the backend says otherwise.

A failure: preserve the work already done

Show four things: the intended outcome, what actually happened, what remains uncertain or unfinished, and a sensible next action. If a send might already have occurred, lead with that fact. Checking the destination is safer than presenting an undifferentiated “Try again.”

Recovery is specific. A file restore names the original path, the recovered version and the conflict behavior. A failed sign-in links to the exact account setup route. A disconnected daemon preserves the draft and explains that task status is currently unknown. The UI must never convert missing status into “Stopped” or “Done.”

Memory: make personalization inspectable

Design memory as editable knowledge, not as a mysterious profile. A useful row could read “Prefer concise project updates,” followed by “From your instruction on 10 Oct,” with Edit and Forget. Provenance and dates are shown only where the underlying record supports them. Keep an inferred suggestion visibly separate from something the person explicitly told FERB.

“Forget” must explain its actual scope. Removing a memory item is not necessarily deleting past transcripts, backups or provider-side records. Likewise, collecting a verdict does not establish that a learning loop is active. Confirm those backend semantics before writing reassuring copy.

Voice: quick to use, easy to correct

Keep existing push-to-talk muscle memory available. Show Listening, Transcribing, Sending and Accepted as distinct states; expose the transcript and a correction route. Text remains a complete alternative. Microphone permission is requested in context, and the listening indication must not be mistaken for background activity sensing. Secret input uses the existing hidden, question-bound route, never the ordinary transcript.

Proactive help: an invitation with a reason

When FERB surfaces a useful observation, say why it appeared and offer a scoped action or dismissal. “You asked me to watch this deadline” is more understandable than an unexplained intervention. Standing permission and suggested action are different. The UI must render the policy’s answer rather than invent new authorization rules.

Key distinction: these are presentation states. They do not replace the daemon’s outcome schema. “Preparing” and “Checking” require real events; “Sending” and “Disconnected” describe client transport. Unknown future event types remain safely visible or ignorable without being treated as success.

Notification behavior

Routine progress: in-app only. Result ready: in-app, with an optional system notification according to settings. A decision: one useful notification linked to the task, respecting system permissions and Focus behavior. Avoid repeated nagging, sensitive previews by default, and alarming red badges for ordinary work. This is a proposed UX policy to reconcile with the current notification implementation and the 0.27 decisions, not a claim that all channels already behave this way.

Layout and accessibility baseline

Use a 4-point spacing foundation; 8–12 points within groups, 20–24 between groups, and generous separation between navigation and work. Prototype a roughly 1000 × 700-point main window with a collapsible sidebar, and a roughly 600-point quick panel. These are starting dimensions to test, not minimum hardware requirements.

Use native hit regions where available and aim for generous 44-point targets for primary custom actions. WCAG’s web target-size criterion is 24 × 24 CSS pixels with stated exceptions; that is a different standard, not a reason to make important controls tiny. [20] [21]

Support keyboard-only operation, full keyboard access, VoiceOver labels and status announcements, text enlargement, high contrast, reduced transparency and reduced motion. Announce meaningful status changes politely; do not read every streamed token. The focus order follows the reading order. Color always has a textual or symbolic partner. A larger text setting may increase window content height or collapse a sidebar; it must not truncate the question or its options.

09 / Visual language

Materials carry meaning. Text carries the task.

Use a visual hierarchy of background, reading surface, navigation, and action. For Daylight, warmth comes from a slightly warm paper surface, ample space and selective editorial type. For Lens, it comes from optical edges and an airy relationship to the desktop. Fieldwork uses contrast and structure; Companion adds rounded form and a small nonhuman presence.

Albers’ work is a useful reminder to evaluate colors relationally. The publisher and official digital edition were reviewed as framing references; no claim of a full plate-by-plate study is made. [3] Actual palette approval should use in-context screens, not isolated swatches.

Role rules: neutral ink carries most text; a single brand accent carries primary interaction; amber denotes attention or uncertainty; red denotes an actual failure or destructive consequence; a label and symbol remain mandatory. Brand olive is not itself proof of success. Avoid low-opacity secondary text on unpredictable glass.

Prototype body text at 15–16 points, controls at 13–14, secondary metadata at 12–13, task headings around 21–24 and occasional editorial headings around 28–32. Use semantic text styles and user text preferences in native implementation. Long responses should remain comfortably readable without turning every chat message into a giant headline.

The accessibility target is at least 4.5:1 for ordinary text and 3:1 for qualifying large text under WCAG’s definition. Evaluate actual composited backgrounds. A passing palette pair does not establish that a translucent or disabled-looking component passes. [15]

Dark mode needs its own surface hierarchy and accent tuning. The boards intentionally hold distinct fixed palettes so the creative directions can be compared; these are not a completed light/dark theme system.

10 / Motion, feedback and sound

Use movement to maintain understanding.

The animate and Emil design-engineering skills informed this proposed motion language. Timings below are starting craft values to test, not psychological laws or measured FERB performance. Rapid keyboard paths should feel immediate.

Do not animate the whole page on every message, auto-scroll someone away from text they are reading, or use a breathing orb as the only indication that work continues. Reserve organic material response for a few places where it clarifies touch or presence.

For a web prototype, favor transform and opacity and measure actual rendering cost. For the native app, prefer platform transitions and materials. Test under agent workload, on a lower-powered Mac, with an external display and on battery. No desktop-wide animation should continue because FERB is waiting on a network call.

Sound is optional and off by default for routine progress. A short, distinguishable completion or attention cue can be offered to people who want it, with a visual equivalent. Voice readouts must not expose private details unexpectedly. Haptics are supplementary and hardware-dependent.

11 / Creative territories

Four ways FERB could feel.

Each board shows the same fictional project-update task. This makes the relationship and layout easier to compare. Material imagery, palettes and typography are creative hypotheses; they are not claims of scientifically proven emotional effects.

Daylight

A place to leave things.

Feeling: considered, warm, orderly. Paper, ceramic, olive and ink.

Interaction: a familiar sidebar and a spacious Now view. Work sits in stable objects. Conversation is always available without dominating the screen.

Distinctive detail: a short editorial greeting gives way to crisp task language; completed work reads like a useful note left on a desk.

Best fit: a broad personal assistant used throughout the day.

Risk: too much cream and serif can drift into a journaling app. Keep the task structure precise and default controls native.

Lens

There when you need it.

Feeling: weightless, immediate, native. Pale glass, metal and reflected light.

Interaction: a transient quick panel over the current workspace, plus a durable full app for history and longer work.

Distinctive detail: the control layer has an optical edge; the review surface stays readable and materially steady.

Best fit: frequent short delegations while working elsewhere.

Risk: ephemeral panels can make work feel lost. An explicit route to the task’s permanent home is essential.

Fieldwork

Work you can account for.

Feeling: precise, durable, composed. Graphite, lichen, machined metal.

Interaction: selected task at left; intent, current state, result and evidence at right. Information stays in predictable places.

Distinctive detail: exact status language and an evidence-first detail view without defaulting to raw logs.

Best fit: complex work, many simultaneous tasks, and users who inspect frequently.

Risk: the assistant may feel like another system to operate. Remove operational density from the everyday default.

Companion

A helping presence.

Feeling: approachable, playful, familiar. Soft ceramic, butter yellow and peach.

Interaction: conversation and a small optional character beside concrete task objects. The character never replaces state text.

Distinctive detail: restrained acknowledgment and warmer empty states; serious decisions remain plain.

Best fit: users who value a recognisable personal presence or frequent voice interaction.

Risk: expressive presentation may overstate understanding or make failures feel flippant. No needy messages, streaks or simulated dependence.

Agency recommendation: start with Daylight as the main app and Lens as the quick panel. Borrow Fieldwork’s inspectability for task details. Keep Companion as a genuine alternative to test, rather than automatically adding a mascot to every screen. A combination should share one type system, one control grammar and one task model.

12 / Decisions, with tradeoffs

Choose the relationship before the decoration.

What remains open: target audience priority; preference for a character; how often users want the full app visible; tolerance for proactive suggestions; first public onboarding task; minimum macOS version. These can be answered through the boards and task tests. They do not prevent this research or concept work.

What should not vary by mood board: truthfulness of outcomes, exact approval scope, stop behavior, readable contrast, keyboard access, and the ability to recover context. Those are the foundation of the experience.

13 / Delivery direction

A native shell around one agent.

Recommended implementation: a SwiftUI main app with AppKit where window behavior, menu-bar presence or a quick panel needs it. Use system typography, standard controls and SF Symbols. Use native Liquid Glass where the selected deployment target supports it; provide standard-material or opaque alternatives for earlier systems and accessibility settings. This is an architectural recommendation, not an implementation completed in this study.

The existing daemon remains responsible for planning, acting, authorization and verification. A client adapter renders JSON-RPC events and outcomes. Keep input and control channels separate. Avoid introducing a second orchestration model in the UI or binding directly to database internals.

Resolve these interface gaps before wiring the polished screens

  1. Durable delivery: chat.status consumes “unseen” state. Persist received results before presenting them; design an explicit acknowledgment protocol if stronger crash or multi-client guarantees are required.
  2. Decision metadata: the inspected _chat_status approval payload contains ID, capability, kind, reason and options, but not expiry, task identity or a structured preview. A rich decision surface needs reliable fields or a safe rendering of the original question. Do not infer recipients from freeform prose for execution.
  3. Context disclosure: show enforced scope, not a decorative attachment list that implies ambient context is excluded. Verify which trace/context records the UI can safely expose.
  4. Memory and routines: inventory the available RPC methods and their semantics before enabling all proposed controls. Memory provenance, suggested memories and notification snoozes may need additional support.
  5. Stop acknowledgments: distinguish the request, revocation, work termination and residual unknown effects. Never claim a network send was undone by stopping.
  6. Model privacy: display actual configured processing destinations, storage and retention accurately. A reassuring lock icon is not an adequate explanation.

Prototype sequence: first prove task continuity and the uncertainty/stop/decision states with fixture events; then connect one real, owner-approved file workflow to the daemon; then add the quick panel and permission onboarding; then memory and routines as their contracts are verified. UI research does not need to change the backend’s current milestone sequence.

This study changed only files under ferb-UI/mac/design-study. It did not change FERB’s source, daemon installation, security rules, browser configuration or personal records.

14 / How we find out if this works

Test relief, understanding and recovery.

Start with a small formative round across people who delegate often, people who are cautious about AI, keyboard-heavy Mac users and people with access needs. A proposed first round of 5–8 participants can uncover problems; it cannot establish population-level preference or statistical superiority.

ScenarioWhat to observeFailure signal
Delegate while writingCan the person submit a task, dismiss FERB, and continue their previous work?They wait watching a spinner or lose their place.
Return after closingCan they find the result and the unresolved decision without rereading the thread?They assume the task was cancelled or repeat the request.
Review a sendCan they state the recipient, account, content and consequence before approval?They approve from a friendly headline without seeing the object.
Unknown send outcomeCan they explain why a retry could duplicate the action?They interpret “not confirmed” as “definitely not sent.”
Stop one taskCan they find the control and predict its scope?They stop everything or believe it undoes completed work.
Correct a memoryCan they find the relevant fact, understand its origin and change it?They must argue with the assistant repeatedly.
Disconnect and reconnectDoes draft preservation and status reconciliation match expectations?The UI invents completion, loses the draft or repeats an action.
Accessibility passKeyboard, VoiceOver, larger text, reduced motion/transparency, light/dark busy wallpapers.A decision or status becomes unavailable, clipped or ambiguous.

Measure time to resume the original work, unnecessary interruptions per completed task, success distinguishing outcome states, ability to find stop, reconstruction effort after absence, recovery success, and perceived control. Ask people to explain what FERB can and cannot do. Task time alone misses the cost of anxious supervision.

Counterbalance the order of the four directions. Keep task content identical. Ask “Which would you use for this task, and why?” before asking which looks nicest. Compare the recommendations again after repeated use; one attractive first impression does not prove sustained comfort.

Acceptance examples for the implementation phase

  • A cancelled task and an unconfirmed send never show the same result language.
  • Closing a window preserves work and reopening restores the correct task state.
  • An old decision cannot approve a changed recipient or expired question.
  • Stop remains keyboard-accessible and dispatches while a reply stream is busy.
  • All important states remain understandable with color removed.
  • Every “Restore” control corresponds to a real recoverable record.
  • No time estimate, progress percentage or “learning” claim appears without supporting state.

These are proposed product acceptance criteria. This turn verified the local design presentation, not the native implementation or a live FERB workflow.
